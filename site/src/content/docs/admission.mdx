description: What a runner must fit before it starts, how runnerd measures jobs, the sizing advice it gives from them, and the auto sizing that applies it.
---

A runner starts only when it fits the slice's `MemoryMax` beside what already runs, and what [the host's memory](#the-hosts-memory) leaves the slice. By default it reserves its profile's `memory`; [the budget check](../configuration/#the-budget-check) holds `slots × memory` to `MemoryMax`. From the jobs' sampled peaks runnerd also gives:

- [Sizing](#sizing) advice for each profile's `memory` and `slots`, always on. [Auto sizing](#auto-sizing) applies it (`[sizing] auto`, off by default; `runnerd init` turns it on).
- [Measured admission](#measured-admission): runners reserve their job's predicted peak, not their cap (`[host] admission = "measured"`, off by default).
- [CPU admission](#cpu-admission): a job waits when the CPUs are spoken for (`[host] cpu_overcommit`, off by default).

[`runnerd simulate`](#simulating) replays your history through the last two first. Keys: [Configuration](../configuration/#every-key).

## What a job's memory is

Containers are sampled every 2 s, so a shorter spike goes unseen. A job's memory is `memory.current` less the file page cache, lower than `docker stats`. The slice's memory, which [measured admission](#measured-admission)'s high-water mark reads, is counted as `docker stats` does. `runnerctl job` shows `sock_throttled`, how often the kernel throttled the container's downloads for want of memory ([memory pressure](../insights/#insights) insight).

## The host's memory

A desktop or local builds take RAM the budget assumed was free, and a host short of memory throttles every runner's sockets. Under either policy runnerd keeps new runners out of that state:

- Every 2 s it reads `MemAvailable` and `/proc/pressure/memory`. The slice may hold what its runners hold now plus `MemAvailable`, less a margin for the host: the larger of 2G and a sixteenth of the host's memory. A runner starts only when its reservation fits that beside the running ones'.
- Nothing starts while the host has under twice the margin available and every task that could run waits for memory 5% of the time or more over 10 s (`full` pressure), until that falls under 1%.
- Once a job waits for the host, the margin grows by half until the host has room again.
- Running jobs are never stopped.
- A start is admitted once more just before it begins, against what runs and was sampled since the plan. A runner that no longer fits waits; the others start, then the tick plans again from the same listings (at most three times).

Without a recent reading only `MemoryMax` bounds starts. A waiting job says why, naming up to three programs outside the slice holding 512M or more:

```text
The host is short of memory: needs 6G; the host has 2G to spare; programs outside CI hold 30G, most java 9.3G and qemu-system-x86 5.8G
```

The status line, the TUI and the dashboard show the host beside the budget (`host: 31G of 64G available; leaves the slice 40G`), and `runnerd doctor` warns when it has less to spare than the smallest `memory`.

### A host whose memory grows

A VM whose hypervisor hands out memory on demand, such as Hyper-V Dynamic Memory, shrinks while idle, so a queued job waits for memory the VM would give. `memory_ceiling` under [`[host]`](../configuration/#host) says how far the host can grow:

```toml
[host]
memory_ceiling = "24G"
```

The slice may then also hold `memory_ceiling` less `MemTotal`, less the margin of the ceiling (`host: 1.48G of 2.12G available, growing to 24G; leaves the slice 21.36G`). Set it to `MemTotal` as the VM reports it at its maximum, lower when other VMs compete for RAM; a ceiling the hypervisor cannot give brings the stalls back.

## Sizing

`runnerctl sizing`, the dashboard's Sizing page and the TUI turn each job's recent peaks into advice for each profile's `memory` and `slots`. A job's recent runs are its last 60 successful ones in 30 days (3 × `min_jobs` when that is more than 20), failed ones included.

- The need is the largest peak plus `margin`, at least 1G for the page cache peaks leave out, rounded up to 256M. It follows the heaviest run, not a percentile: the cap kills a job that goes over. Under `min_jobs` jobs there is no advice.
- A run met its cap when its container's own limit ran out of memory (`oom` in `memory.events.local`); it counts at least its cap. A peak near the cap without `oom` is not a need for more.
- A job with such a run, or one killed for memory, stays on its cap until 3 runs in a row succeed clear of it; the profile is not lowered meanwhile.
- The advice lowers `memory` when the need is a tenth or more under it. Under `caps`, if jobs waited a minute or more (p95, [how a wait is counted](#how-a-wait-is-counted)) or the slots were busy half the time, it also adds as many slots as the memory now holds.
- Under `measured`, advice never changes `slots`; the advice line says when more would help.

[`[sizing]`](../configuration/#sizing) sets `margin` and `min_jobs`. Advice is written only when applied: `runnerctl sizing apply <profile>`, `a` in the TUI, or the Sizing page's button. It goes through the same check as any config and keeps to the profile's `min_memory`, `max_memory`, `max_slots` and [`memory_ceiling`](#a-host-whose-memory-grows). `runnerd doctor` warns when a profile's jobs need more than its `memory`.

### How a wait is counted

A job's wait runs from when it was queued, or when runnerd first ran if later, to its start. Time runnerd was down is left out; sizing also leaves out time spent waiting for [the host's memory](#the-hosts-memory).

Under `measured`, only a wait for a slot alone adds slots: jobs one more slot would have started, with memory and CPU to spare (`jobs waited 300s for a slot (p95) while memory and CPU had room`).

### Auto sizing

With `auto = true` runnerd sizes each profile's `memory`, `slots` and `cpus`. At start and every 5 minutes it writes each change into the config as `runnerctl sizing apply` does, with the same budget check.

- **Memory** follows the advice, between `min_memory` and `max_memory` and under `memory_ceiling` less the mirror. A lowering moves at most `max_step` percent at a time. A raise comes only from a run that met the cap, straight to the need.
- **A lowering is taken back** at the next look when a run meets the lowered cap, or a job that succeeded before fails 3 times in a row at it. For 30 days no lowering goes under it.
- **Slots under `caps`:** one more when jobs waited a minute or more at p95, or the slots were busy half the time, while every profile's caps fit `MemoryMax`, up to `max_slots`.
- **Slots under `measured`:** as many more as waited at once, at most `max_step` percent, when jobs waited a minute or more at p95 for a slot alone. Slots cost disk, not memory: they go up only while each keeps 2G of the quarter of Docker's disk that all Docker stores share. A profile whose slots stayed 2 or more above its use for a week loses one.
- **CPUs** follow average use and throttling, not peaks. A run is held back when the CPU quota throttled it in 15% or more of its run. A job with 3 runs averaging 70% of the cap gets more `cpus`, under either policy; under `measured`, 3 held-back runs earn a raise too. Raises go up to the slice's CPUs (under `caps`, the CPU budget), while each one helps. Under `caps` they come down when `min_jobs` runs say so and none was held back.

When a bound or the budget blocks a change, `runnerctl sizing` says `No slot raise:` or `No CPU raise` and why. A raise waits `raise_interval` (1 hour) after the last change, a lowering `interval` (1 hour). A change is never written over a file you edited on disk: until runnerd runs with your edit (restart it), auto sizing changes nothing and logs why. Each change is recorded in the job history, sent as a `sizing.changed` event, and listed by `runnerctl sizing`. `runnerctl sizing undo <profile>` writes the old values back; with `auto` on, a later change can undo the undo, so bound the profile to keep it.

## Measured admission

`admission = "measured"` has each runner reserve its job's predicted peak instead of its cap:

```toml
[host]
admission = "measured"

[profiles.node]
memory = "7G"   # still the container's hard limit
slots = 8       # 8 × 7G may exceed MemoryMax
```

- A job (repository, workflow and job name) is predicted from its last 20 runs, or `min_jobs` when that is more, counting any that failed among them: their p95 peak plus `margin`, rounded up to 256M. A run that met its cap counts at least its cap.
- With fewer runs: the larger of its profile's p95 and its own largest peak, plus the margin; with none, its profile's largest peak plus the margin. Once 2 of its runs succeeded, one copy of the job at a time reserves its own largest peak plus the margin instead; a copy queued or running beside another takes the larger. Without `min_jobs` runs of the profile, the cap.
- A job whose run met its cap or was killed for memory goes back on its cap until 3 runs in a row succeed clear of it, 7 days at most.
- A runner reserves the largest prediction among the queued jobs its labels match ([split light and heavy jobs](../configuration/#split-light-and-heavy-jobs)), then its own job's once known.
- A running runner counts at the larger of what it reserved and what it last used, or its cap without a recent sample.
- Nothing starts while the slice uses 90% of `MemoryMax`, counted [as `docker stats` does](#what-a-jobs-memory-is).

The caps still stop a container that goes over them, but several jobs going over their predictions at once can fill the slice, and the kernel then kills a process in it. `runnerctl job <id>` says what a runner reserved and why.

### Simulating

`runnerd simulate` replays the last 30 days through each policy, every job queued when it was and running as long as it ran. It changes nothing. This history on 8 CPUs, node's slots raised to 8:

```text
$ runnerd simulate --memory-max 33G --slots node=8 --cpus 8 --cpu-overcommit 1 --cpu-overcommit 1.5
POLICY    SLOTS                   WAIT P50  WAIT P95  WAIT MAX      MEAN AT ONCE    PEAKS   UNDER  HELD    CPU CPU HELD
caps      jvm 2, node 6                 0s     4m19s     7m38s       57s       5   18.95G       0     0    6.2        -
measured  jvm 3, node 8                 0s     1m55s     3m20s       18s       8   24.35G       4     0      7        -
cpu ×1    jvm 3, node 8                 0s     2m30s     4m21s       32s       8   24.16G       4     0      7      281
cpu ×1.5  jvm 3, node 8                 0s     2m08s     4m21s       21s       8   24.35G       4     0      7        8
```

- `PEAKS`: every running job's peak added up at the busiest moment. `UNDER`: jobs that peaked above what admission reserved. `HELD`: starts the high-water mark held back.
- `cpu ×N`: a CPU budget of N times the slice's CPUs. `CPU`: the most the jobs' average CPUs added up to. `CPU HELD`: starts the budget held back.

Options: `--memory-max`, `--slots PROFILE=N`, `--since 7d`, `--cpus`, and `--cpu-overcommit`, repeated to compare factors.

## CPU admission

`cpu_overcommit` under `[host]` adds a CPU budget, the slice's CPUs times the factor, and a runner starts only when what it reserves of that fits too:

```toml
[host]
admission = "measured"
cpu_overcommit = 1.0
```

- The slice's CPUs are its `AllowedCPUs`, or the host's, fewer when its `CPUQuota` allows less, read from systemd.
- Under `measured` a runner reserves its job's average CPU, predicted from the history as memory is, with no margin: the factor is the margin. Under `caps` it reserves its profile's `cpus`.

A job that had a slot and memory but not CPU waits; after 2 minutes no younger job starts first. The status line says so (`CPU 30 of 32 CPUs reserved; a job waits for CPU`).

Averages are far under peaks, so on a host with many CPUs they admit nearly everything; on 8 CPUs ([above](#simulating)) the budget holds jobs. Start at `1.0`: peaks that land together cost a short slowdown, not a kill.

### Sharing the CPUs

Linux shares the slice's CPUs between containers in proportion to each profile's `cpus`, and no container gets more than its `cpus`. A low `CPUWeight` on the slice, such as 20, leaves the desktop its share.
